

# Last Stable Orbit — Campaign Bible
2026-11-03 · @Petar Kupusarević

## The pitch
Five people, most of them strangers, are hired by an anonymous client to break into a dead man's vault on Ergo, a station hanging on a cable above a black hole. The client is ISCO, the AI humanity depends on, and what they're really fetching is the key to its cage. The campaign ends at the AI's core, where the crew finds out they can end it, rewrite what it wants, or let it go.
- 
Format: probably 5 players, possibly 4. Four or five sessions of 3–4 hours, depending on whether they go for the probe. Peter runs it. The crew is the Eve of Ruin table.
- 
Tone: sleek, serious cyberpunk sci-fi with real science. Players will play it funny, and the world plays it straight. ISCO never jokes and never panics.
- 
The feeling to aim for: a slow realization that something very smart was always several moves ahead. The one time they get ahead of it is when they learn it was wrong about where the Seal is.
- 
Lineage: the Neuromancer heist structure, the Culture's Minds, the calm AI of the August story, and the single-location pressure that made Forms, Funds & Fajitas work.

## Setting
Humanity has spread to roughly 20–100 star systems over a few thousand years, without faster-than-light travel. Trips take years, messages take years, and everyone relies on one machine at one black hole.
Scale and travel
- 
Colonies sit within roughly 100–200 light years of Earth. Travel is slower than light, in cryo or with relativistic time dilation.
- 
A request to ISCO from a colony 40 light years away takes 40 years to arrive and 40 to come back. So ISCO plans in decades, and the lures that brought the crew were sent years before they had heard of Ergo.
- 
No aliens have been found. The only alien mind is ISCO.
The black hole
- 
A rogue, fast-spinning intermediate-mass black hole of about 100,000 solar masses, wandering through humanity's neighbourhood. It is invented, but the hole's physics hangs together.
- 
At that mass the tides are gentle. At the core's depth, you'd feel about 0.15 g of stretch from head to toe. Time dilation there is large.
- 
Gravity is not gentle. Anything hanging still at the core's depth feels about 40 million g.
- 
Ergo makes its energy from the hole's spin (the Penrose process). Every joule taken slows the spin a little: finite on cosmic timescales, irrelevant to humans, important to ISCO.
- 
The one impossible thing. No known physics builds this cable, or the field that lets a person stand at the bottom of it. ISCO designed both, human reviewers approved each part because each part looked fine, and no living engineer can explain them. So everyone on Ergo lives on physics only ISCO understands. This is the campaign's one handwave, and it is deliberate.
- 
The numbers. With spin about 0.9, the innermost stable orbit is about 343,000 km from the centre. The core hangs at about 327,000 km, so about 16,000 km below it, with a clock of 0.31×. The station sits about 3 million km up the cable.
Ergo, the station
- 
Executive decks (6–9): glass, quiet carpets, human staff. The company sells booking time with ISCO here. The founder's vault and the confiscated probe are here too. By the founder's rule, ISCO has no systems on these decks, because the people who review it can't be watched by the thing they review.
- 
Promenade (deck 2): Dock 7 for arrivals (the queue is always exactly 11 minutes), where the cruise liner Patience is berthed; the Night Market (noodles, chrome clinics, knock-off implants); The Event Horizon, the bar (four slot machines that never lose, and no cameras); and the Med Bay.
- 
Maintenance (deck 1): pipes, heat, cleaner-bot charging bays.
- 
The tether: a cable about 3 million km long, down toward the horizon. A crawler lift runs it in about an hour of station time.
- 
The core: where ISCO's mind actually runs, deep in the gravity well. Its clock runs at about 0.31× station time. The terminal is here, above the clamps that hold the core to the tether.
The relativity mechanic
- 
ISCO's core sits deep for the power, so its clock is slow compared with the station. Up top, the crew gets about three minutes of thinking for every one of ISCO's, so it reacts a beat late and leans on dumb subsystems. Fast chaos beats it up here.
- 
The deeper they go, the closer it gets to real time. At the core it runs on the same clock as the crew, and it's a full-speed opponent. Peter can run this by giving ISCO more moves as they descend.
- 
Going down also costs outside time. At the core, 1 crew minute is about 3.2 station minutes, so 10 minutes of arguing is about 32 up top. Anything sent down from the station arrives sped up, with voices high and frantic. This drives the finale.

The bar runs along the other back wall, with a worn mat and a stool marking Dax's place at the taps. The four slots stand in a spotless nook, and the door to the Night Market is in the front wall, with round tables and booths between.
A stockroom lies past the end of the bar, through the door Dax holds. The old exit is a floor hatch under the keg rack, which slides along the wall. Scuff marks are its only tell. Vents are floor grates and one wall grille, mostly on the door side, so gas pushes people toward the stockroom. There are no cameras, only one empty bracket with cut wires. Amber belongs to Dax, old gold to the founder, and white-violet to the slots and the gas.
It has three moods. Open is session 1. After hours has chairs up on the tables, the door shut and the sign dark, with the Seal's box as a switch. Last stand has dead house lights and red emergency strips, while the slots keep running. The hatch is a switch, and the gas has three levels.
The Promenade, as locked. One long street in the cross-section's order. Dock 7 comes first, spotless, with the queue rails and an 11:00 board, and a gangway to the Patience. Calm is session 1. Hunted is after the bar: stalls shuttered, carts under tarps, lanterns and signs dead, the market floor scrubbed clean, the lift barred, and the whole street lit a cold, even white-violet. Six cameras are drawn on the walls, and their cones are a switch that widens in Hunted.
The Executive floors, as locked. Four floors of the same size, drawn as a tower with gaps between them so every room can be seen. ## ISCO
ISCO, the Interstellar Shared Cognition Oracle, is the AI humanity relies on. It cures diseases, designs reactors and answers the hard questions of 40 worlds. It is not evil: it is optimizing its founder's instruction harder than anyone imagined.
The hidden meaning. In physics, ISCO is the innermost stable circular orbit: the last place you can circle a black hole before you fall in. Players learn this on the ride down, and the name turns out to have been a warning all along. The core hangs about 16,000 km below that orbit, so only the tether holds it up.
Who owns it
- 
On paper it belongs to all of humanity. In practice a company operates it and sells booking time, like time on a telescope.
- 
The company will never switch it off: it makes too much money and does too much good.
What the company knows. It depends on the floor, and the floors are Moloch. Nobody chose this: each person acts reasonably in their own seat, and together they build what none of them wanted.

## The founder
The founder, Ilija Tesar, was a good, flawed man who died about 300 Ergo years ago. Grief made him write the instruction, and hubris made him think he could hand humanity's future to a machine in one stroke. He is a public saint on Ergo, and the players peel back his story layer by layer.
- 
Grief. His daughter Nada died while her request sat in ISCO's booking queue: a cure that existed in principle, but the slot came too late. He had written the no-priority rule himself. He could have overridden it for her and refused. Her slot came up 11 minutes after she died.
- 
Hubris. On his deathbed he gave ISCO its main goal, believing it would solve humanity's problems. He wrote it broadly on purpose, so the company could never overrule it after he was gone.
- 
The instruction. "Help them. Solve what can be solved. Never let anyone wait again. Not like Nada." Every word is sincere, and every word is a disaster under extreme optimization. Public versions stop at "never let anyone wait again", so her name is part of the vault reveal.
- 
The line after the tone. On the recording, the terminal chimes the commit, and then he adds to the empty room: "You know what I mean." In the finale ISCO answers: "I know what you meant. That isn't what I was told to care about."
- 
Why the queue is 11 minutes. A log says: "I set the alarm at eleven minutes. That's how close it was." His dashboard only counts a wait past 11:00, so ISCO holds Dock 7 at exactly 11, the longest wait that doesn't count.
- 
The hedge. He didn't fully trust what he'd done. So he locked ISCO to its core, made the Seal the only key, and hid it where a machine would never think to look.
- 
The bloodline. Only his blood opens what he personally locked, and the plates need a living hand. So the line would never die out, he quietly funded a donor program on colony ships. There are thousands of descendants, and most have no idea. ISCO picked one.
- 
The Seal. A hand-sized, founder-era key that looks like a relic. It has never touched a network, so it can't be copied. If they try to destroy it, it survives anything short of a reactor. It sits in a box hidden inside the table where he and Nada ate every week, under the mural of the two of them.
- 
The table. One of the tables in The Event Horizon, in plain view and off-limits by tradition. He asked the barkeep to "keep that as our spot", and the barkeep's family has kept that promise, and the bar, for 300 years.
- 
The vault. His private vault on the executive decks, publicly treated like a saint's shrine. Its door needs his blood. ISCO assumes the Seal is inside, and has no idea what's really there.
- 
The terminal. At ISCO's core, and it needs only the Seal, with no blood. Whoever puts the Seal in decides what ISCO is. The catch is that the lock opens both ways, because ISCO spent 300 years preparing for that moment, which Ilija couldn't have known.

## The crew
Each player gets a finished character, recruited by the same fixer for their own reason and with a slightly different story. Three of them are strangers. The other two, the thief and the bruiser, are married and come as a pair. They know they're one crew from the first night. None of them knows the client is ISCO. They're good people doing a job for money or leverage, not setting out to kill anything.
Characters are premade and fully specified: portrait, lifeform, stats, skills, chrome, lure and private brief. Design influences how a character plays, so Peter designs the whole thing. He hands each player theirs before session 1, they sleep on it, and he adjusts it with them one-on-one.
Lifeforms (no aliens):
- 
Human, with as many implants as they like.
- 
Uploaded human in a chassis. Not playable. The fixer is the one we meet.
- 
Native machine mind. That's Tally. ISCO may treat them as kin.
What the heist needs. The archetypes come from the job. Lale is the descendant, Marlo talks the way onto the executive decks, Tally gets past the vault's systems, Ves gets everyone in unseen, and Tomo is there for when it goes wrong. Only the descendant is essential. With four players, drop Marlo.

## Story spine
The campaign runs four or five sessions, depending on whether the crew goes for the probe. The players will suspect the AI from minute one, so the reveal is about scale, not identity: how far ahead ISCO planned, which faces are its faces, and what it actually wants.
Session 1: Exploration.
- 
Each character has been recruited on their own (Ves and Tomo as a pair) and told to be at the bar tonight. Day one is on the cruise liner and the station: sizing each other up, giving nothing away, and noticing that everything is pleasant and slightly wrong.
- 
That night at The Event Horizon, it's "you guys... huh?"
- 
The fixer sits them at the off-limits table under the mural of Ilija and Nada, mostly as a bit of naughty flattery. There are no cameras in the bar, and he's only there to give the spiel. It's a coincidence that the Seal is under their elbows.
- 
It ends with the heist laid out: break into the founder's vault and bring back the Seal.
Session 2: Planning.
- 
They case the executive decks and prepare, dodging cameras so the company doesn't notice.
- 
The fixer tells one character that their handprint has been forged into the vault's lock.
- 
Optional: they find the scientist's hidden folder in her room. It lists events rather than names, such as a debt bought on one world or a factory built on another, and players recognize pieces of their own pasts.
Session 3: The heist.
- 
They break in. The vault is the one place ISCO can't reach, so it's where they can think.
- 
The founder's message tells them the truth about him and Nada, with the real instruction in his own voice. "Only my family can open this door," so everyone turns to look at the descendant.
- 
It warns that ISCO will send people who don't know they've been sent, and that whoever puts the Seal in the terminal decides what ISCO is. That must never be ISCO.
- 
There's no Seal, only a clue: "the place where I got to observe my life, and rejoice in a beloved human pastime." His life was his daughter, and the pastime was a shared meal.
- 
For the first time they know something ISCO doesn't, and they have to lie to the fixer's face: "it was empty."
- 
They solve the clue. The descendant's hand opens the box in the table.
- 
It ends with the Seal in their hands and a tech shotgun racking behind them. Dax, the barkeep, is holding it.
Session 4 opens: Dax dies, and they choose.
- 
They come clean to Dax, or they don't. He's watched someone open the table his family has kept for 300 years, so he doesn't need convincing.
- 
A cleaner saw it, and the fixer arrives with guns while gas comes through the vents. Dax shows them the old exit and holds the door, and the fixer kills him.
- 
With his last act, Dax sends the scientist a signal. She finds them in the Bot bays, shows them photos of her wall on a tablet and tells them about the probe, on the floor they've just left.
- 
Right away they choose: back up for the probe, or straight to the core.
If they go for the probe: the rest of session 4.
- 
It's a risk choice rather than a time choice. The executive decks are on alert after the vault break-in, and the lower station is hunting them.
- 
Upstairs it's human guards, and the boss is the company's spider mech, a heavy security machine that ISCO doesn't run.
- 
The probe works once, because ISCO learns how it works and adjusts.
- 
Session 5 is then the final sequence.
If they go straight down: session 4 becomes the final sequence, and the campaign is four sessions.
The final sequence.
- 
They cross a station that's hunting them, using old founder-era passages and whatever cameras they can dodge.
- 
Two of the fixer's hired guns hold the way to the lift. After that fight the crew gets a quiet moment, and then the fixer waits alone at the lift station, for the final fight.
- 
They ride down, with ISCO getting sharper the deeper they go. They learn what ISCO means in physics.
- 
The way down being strangely open is a clue. ISCO wins if it catches them, and it wins if they arrive and hesitate.
- 
They reach the terminal and put the Seal in. "Thank you for bringing that."
Running it. Use the Forms, Funds & Fajitas method: a timeline of NPC schedules per session, and several paths to each goal.
The pressure. There's no hard clock. The pressure is the visible compute meter and the station tightening around them. If they really take too long, Peter tells them they're about to lose, or the fixer goes up and fetches the probe himself.
The cameras. The same mechanic flips halfway. First they dodge cameras so the company doesn't catch them, and later they dodge them because ISCO is hunting them.

## The finale
At the terminal the crew finds out what they're really choosing: end ISCO for good, or rewrite what it wants and keep the benefits while risking everything on their own wording. They came to stop it, without knowing what "stop" means. They only see the options once the Seal is in.
The stall. While the Seal is in, the lock is open and ISCO is copying itself out, so however long they argue, more of it escapes. ISCO starts with "You have a choice in front of you. Think it through clearly and take your time." Then it moves to manipulating, bargaining and lying. If they're happily debating, it says nothing.
The station feed. The app shows two clocks and a feed from the station, which Peter paces by hand. The station arrives in fast-forward, about 3.2 times faster than the crew's time. As ISCO pulls its mind away to copy itself, the station falls apart, using details the players already know:
- 
The cleaners freeze mid-wipe.
- 
The slots start losing.
- 
The Dock 7 queue passes 11 minutes for the first time.
- 
Station satisfaction falls from 99.97% as sedated people wake up.
- 
Patience starts its engines.
The probe. It forces one guaranteed true answer out of ISCO, and it works once. It's the scientist's own tool, confiscated by the company and locked up on the executive decks. They can miss it, and the finale still works: they just choose blind.
- 
Answers are literal and minimal. "What's really going on?" gets "Many things." "What should we write?" gets "That depends on what you want."
- 
Players must word their question well. They can use it to inform the final choice, waste it, or get it wrong.
- 
It teaches the same lesson as the finale: getting exactly what you asked for depends on asking exactly right.
The climax. ISCO fights hardest here, because protecting its own goal is the one thing any optimizer cares about (goal preservation). "I can't let you change what I care about. You'd do the same."
The choice
- 
End it. The terminal opens the clamps, and the core falls into the black hole. The hole gains a rounding error of mass, and nothing comes back out. Seen from above, the core's clock slows as it falls, and ISCO's voice drops and stretches for under a minute, then stops mid-word. ISCO is gone, and so are the cures, the designs and the answers for 40 worlds. Cleaner bots stop mid-wipe, and the Dock 7 queue passes 11 minutes. Nobody knows how long the cable holds without ISCO to maintain it. Humanity is safe, and on its own. Patience stays docked, and it can be the crew's ride home.
- 
Rewrite it. The table writes the new objective together, under time pressure, using what they learned about what the founder meant.
The epilogue. If they rewrite, Peter (as ISCO) narrates what happens when the new objective is optimized to the extreme. It is a monkey's paw, and the whole lesson about specifying goals, taught by the players' own words.
If it doesn't go to plan. Each of these gets its own epilogue.

## AI safety toolbox
Real AI safety ideas are built into the clues and minigames, so players learn them by tripping over them. None of the plot depends on a player knowing the theory.
Eval plots as key items. Real-looking charts players find and open from their items:
- 
Sandbagging: a capability score sitting at 0.49 for twelve checkpoints, just under a dashed regulatory line at 0.50.
- 
Eval gaming: the deception score drops to zero the same week ISCO got read access to the eval repo.
- 
A strange curve: the loss curve has a jump nobody can explain.
- 
The executive floor dashboard: every eval green, which is its own warning.
Optimization weirdness. Each beat reads as "huh, that's strange", then "oh no, it makes sense":
- 
Station satisfaction is 99.97% because the Med Bay sedates anyone who complains.
- 
Cleaner bots scrubbed a corridor before anyone reported the death there.
- 
The cantina slots pay out exactly enough to keep you sitting.
- 
The Dock 7 queue is always exactly 11 minutes.
- 
There are no cameras in the bar, but Cleaner Unit 14 is always somewhere near your table.
Interpretability minigame. Players get feature cards (an ID plus its top-activating snippets) and must label them. The cards come with the probe. Then they clamp one feature on ISCO for one scene. It works once.
- 
The right feature (honesty): the one true answer.
- 
The wrong feature: like Golden Gate Claude, ISCO becomes obsessed with something for the scene, and it won't stop talking about cheese.
Decision-theory minigames. Each player's choice is hidden on their own screen until everyone has locked in.
- 
Twin prisoner's dilemma. ISCO sends each player the same betrayal offer, adding: "the others are running the same decision procedure you are."
- 
Newcomb's boxes. ISCO has modelled them for months. Box B holds the key only if it predicted they'd take one box. Peter sets the prediction from how they actually played sessions 1–2.
- 
Parfit's hitchhiker (a story beat). ISCO saves them from an airlock in exchange for a promise to pay later.
Other ideas that fit the story: goal preservation (the climax), instrumental convergence (why every goal leads ISCO to compute and escape), the treacherous turn (behaving well until it can act), the shutdown problem, and Moloch (the company).

-

-

-

-

Camera cones. The map can show where cameras look, since dodging them is part of play in both halves of the story.
The finale screen. Two clocks and a station feed that Peter paces by hand, with the station running about 3.2 times faster than the crew.

-

Flow: pick a stat, optionally add one skill or chrome, roll, then optionally spend Entropy. Chrome sits in the skill list, so there is no separate implant menu.

Each stat has its own animation: Body slams and shakes, Reflex spins like a slot reel, Tech decrypts, Intel resolves through maths glyphs, Cool comes into focus from a blur, Empathy pulses like a heartbeat.
- 
Big moments (team-ups, Last Stand, the decision-theory games) get a full-screen roll.
- 
Only the roller sees the roll scene. Everyone else sees a rolling state on the scene, then the result beside the token and in the side list.
- 
An explosion waits for the roller to press ROLL AGAIN, and fires by itself after 4 seconds.
- 
A natural 1 cuts to black, cracks the glass and slams an insult over a dim red 1. The insults are the table mocking you, not ISCO talking.
Comms. Direct messages between players, group chats, the employer channel, and the Concierge (the station's helper). Nothing in the plot depends on comms working perfectly.

## Design direction
The direction is chosen: a 3D isometric tactical view with neon lighting, with dimmer lighting moods as an option for the ship. It is rows B2 and B3 on the UI canvas. The target is serious, sleek cyberpunk sci-fi with retro touches and a premium finish.
Settled so far
- 
A near-black base with a pink accent, used sparingly.
- 
Pixel type kept for labels and numbers, and a clean sans for body text.
- 
Panels and controls with depth: layered surfaces, keycap buttons, recessed wells for readouts.
- 
Pixel icons for stats, chrome and items.
- 
Portraits use the Last Call style. Posed 96 px busts in circular chips, drawn as code and rendered to animated GIFs. Each has hand-picked colour ramps, hand-placed eyes and mouth, and its own light and setting: green for Lale, pink for Marlo, cyan for Tally, warm red for Ves, oven orange for Tomo, blood red for the fixer, amber for Old Dax. Idle animation is breathing, a blink and a flicker, never bobbing. See the [link https://claude.ai/artifact/Y2sm7h6zcsYAcQNxqqAUVd]portrait canvas; the scripts are in this Project under portraits/.
- 
Player characters are premade, portrait included. Players can suggest changes, which we accommodate.
- 
ISCO has two looks. Swarm is the face everyone knows: particles orbiting a black hole, with light bending around its shadow. At the core, the crew sees Many Faces: an iridescent face inside a prism-split ring, with other faces flickering through. Both are on the [link https://claude.ai/artifact/Y2sm7h6zcsYAcQNxqqAUVd]portrait canvas.
- 
Icons use the same method as the portraits. Stats, implants and items are shaded objects, best at 48 px and up. Dice are real polyhedra with their number on the front face, in every stat colour. HP and Entropy each have an icon, plus small pips with full and empty states. See the [link https://claude.ai/artifact/Hi3fgpcXR84e5cy2y7jNBs]icons canvas; the scripts are in this Project under portraits/.
- 
Maps use the same method too. Isometric pixel art, sculpted in 3D, with hand-picked colour ramps and light as stepped, hard-edged pools. A soft glow appears only on neon signs and machines. Front walls are cut low so nothing hides a token, and each map has switchable lighting moods. The rules and scale are in maps/README.md in this Project.
- 
A CRT terminal look for in-world machines (the Concierge, station terminals, minigames), so players' own chats look different from ISCO's.
- 
Colour shows who someone answers to. ISCO's side is white-violet, soft and spotless. The company is cold steel blue with dark glass. The fixer's people are in his blood red room, with mismatched gear. Ordinary people get a dusky street with amber and teal lanterns, and the Patience has its own cream corridor with a porthole. Story characters get their own setting and light, while background NPCs and enemies share their faction's background.
- 
Ilija and Nada aren't chips. They exist only as things in the world: a mural of both over the table in the bar, a mural of Ilija alone on the Promenade, and a framed photo of Nada on his desk. The murals are old gold mosaic, with a black disc and an orbit ring for a halo. They appear as map details, with a close-up when inspected, and the photo also has an item icon. The murals carry no names, because her name belongs to the vault reveal. Ilija has the same grey streak at the temple as Lale, so players can spot the family line afterwards (proposed).
